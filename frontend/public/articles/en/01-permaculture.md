# What permaculture is, and Holmgren's 12 principles

Most people think "permaculture" means organic gardening done well. It's an understandable mix-up, but it misses the target. Permaculture is not a way of growing: it is **a method for designing systems** — gardens, farms, homes, communities — by copying the logic that makes natural ecosystems work.

The term was coined in 1978 by Australians **Bill Mollison and David Holmgren** as a contraction of *permanent agriculture* (later *permanent culture*). The underlying idea is simple and radical: a mature forest needs no gardener. It fertilises, sows, defends and adapts itself. If we could design human systems with the same intelligence, they would stop degrading and start improving on their own.

## Three ethics before the principles

Beneath everything lie three ethics — the compass that separates permaculture from a mere bag of garden tricks:

- **Earth Care**
- **People Care**
- **Fair Share** — redistribute surplus instead of hoarding it.

## Holmgren's 12 principles

In 2002, in *Permaculture: Principles and Pathways Beyond Sustainability*, Holmgren formulated the **12 design principles** that are still the reference standard today. Here they are, with what they really mean.

**1. Observe and interact.** Before changing anything, watch it for a full cycle. Where does the sun hit in January? Where does water pool? The best design comes from observation, not from an idea.

**2. Catch and store energy.** Sun, water, organic matter: gather them when they're abundant so you have them when they're scarce. A cistern, a compost heap, a tree are all "batteries".

**3. Obtain a yield.** A system that produces nothing useful in the short term won't sustain itself. This is the principle that forbids endless postponing: yield first, then scale.

**4. Apply self-regulation and accept feedback.** Listen to error signals and correct early. A system that ignores feedback collapses.

**5. Use and value renewable resources and services.** Prefer what regenerates (the work of bees, nitrogen from legumes) over what is consumed only once.

**6. Produce no waste.** In nature, one organism's waste is another's food. Fallen leaves, manure, pruning residues are not waste: they are inputs in the wrong place.

**7. Design from patterns to details.** First the overall structure (where the water goes, where the sun is, how you move through it), then the particulars. The common mistake is starting from details.

**8. Integrate rather than segregate.** Put elements in relationship: hens that fertilise the garden, the tree that shades the pond. The right connections do the work for you.

**9. Use small and slow solutions.** Small systems are easier to maintain and to correct. Slow growth is more stable than fast growth.

**10. Use and value diversity.** Monoculture is fragile by definition. Diversity is the ecosystem's insurance policy.

**11. Use edges and value the marginal.** The point where two systems meet — wood and meadow, land and water — is the richest in life and opportunity. What seems marginal is often the most productive.

**12. Creatively use and respond to change.** Change is not an enemy: it is raw material. A good design doesn't resist change, it uses it.

## Why we work this way

At Humus Sapiens these aren't posters on a wall. The food forest is principles 8 and 10 put into practice: different layers of plants supporting one another. The hens in the design are principle 6. Staying small and growing slowly is principle 9 — and it's also why we don't add a new activity before making the ones we have work.

The most useful thing you can take home requires no hectare of land: it's **principle 1**. Before changing anything — a balcony, a habit, a job — observe it for a full cycle. Almost always the solution was already there, visible, waiting for someone to look at it.
