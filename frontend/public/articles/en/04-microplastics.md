# Microplastics: what's happening in the world?

In recent years microplastics have gone from a niche topic for oceanographers to a discovery that concerns your blood — literally. But around the subject there is as much solid science as exaggeration. It's worth separating what we know for certain from what we're still studying, because the difference is enormous and it matters.

## What they are, exactly

**Microplastics**: fragments of plastic smaller than **5 millimetres**. Below the micrometre we speak of **nanoplastics**, even more insidious because they can cross biological barriers.

Where do they come from? A tiny share are produced already small (the old microbeads in cosmetics). For the most part they arise from the **fragmentation** of larger plastic and from everyday wear: tyres wearing on asphalt, **synthetic textiles** releasing microfibres with every wash, packaging crumbling. You don't need to throw plastic into the sea to produce it: it happens in the washing machine and in traffic.

## Where they've ended up: everywhere

This is the chapter of **verified facts**, and they are striking on their own, without any need for emphasis. Microplastics have been found:

- in the **deepest ocean trenches** and in Arctic snow;
- in the **air** we breathe, indoors and outdoors;
- in **drinking water**, bottled and tap, and in common foods (sea salt, fish, honey, beer);
- in the **human body**: a 2022 study (Leslie et al., *Environment International*) detected microplastics for the first time in the **blood** of adult donors. Other research has found them in the placenta, lungs and tissues.

The picture is clear: exposure is widespread and continuous. On this there is no debate.

## What we (don't) know about health

Here honesty is needed, and it's the part that sensational headlines skip. **That microplastics are in our bodies is a fact. That they cause a specific harm, and how much, is still the subject of research.**

The **World Health Organization**, in a 2019 assessment on drinking water, concluded that the available evidence did not yet allow a clear health risk to be established — while strongly calling for more research. Since then studies have multiplied and signals of concern have grown, but the step from *"they are present"* to *"they cause this disease"* is not yet robustly demonstrated.

Two useful cautions to keep in mind:

- The often-cited figure that we ingest **"5 grams of plastic a week, like a credit card"** (2019 study commissioned by WWF) went viral, but has been **contested on methodological grounds**: use it with caution, not as truth carved in stone.
- The absence of proof of harm **is not** proof of safety. It means: we are running an experiment on ourselves whose outcome we do not know. The precautionary principle exists precisely for situations like this.

## What Europe is doing

The EU did not wait for absolute certainty to act. In 2023, under the **REACH** regulation, it adopted a **restriction on microplastics intentionally added** to products (from cosmetics to detergents), with a progressive phase-out. It's part of the same regulatory direction concerning food-contact materials and the "forever chemicals" (PFAS). The trajectory is set: what is tolerated today will be restricted tomorrow.

## What you can do, without obsessing

You don't live in a bubble, and turning the subject into anxiety is counterproductive. But some choices reduce exposure concretely, and they're also common sense:

- Avoid **heating food in plastic** (heat increases release): use glass or ceramic.
- Prefer **glass, terracotta, steel containers** for storing food.
- Cut down on **plastic-bottled water** when you can.
- Wash **synthetic textiles** less often and at full load; microfibre filters exist.

It's not a solution to the global problem — that comes through regulation and the supply chain. But it's the margin of control you have, and it's not nil.
