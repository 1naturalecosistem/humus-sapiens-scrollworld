# The global pollinator crisis

Roughly **one forkful in three** of the food you eat exists thanks to an animal that moved pollen from one flower to another. It's not a figure of speech: it's the estimate of the intergovernmental science platform **IPBES**, the biodiversity equivalent of the IPCC. And those animals are in trouble. It's worth understanding how much, why, and what actually works — without alarmism, which burns attention while changing nothing.

## The numbers that matter

In 2016 IPBES published the first global assessment of pollinators. The key figures, still the reference today:

- About **75% of the world's leading food crops** depend, at least in part, on animal pollination.
- It concerns around **35% of the volume** of global agricultural production — above all fruit, vegetables, nuts, seeds.
- The annual economic value of pollination is estimated between **235 and 577 billion dollars**.
- Regionally, a significant share of pollinator species is at risk: up to **40% of invertebrate pollinator species** — mainly wild bees and butterflies — are threatened with extinction in some areas.

A point that often escapes notice: the honey bee is just one of some **20,000 bee species** worldwide, almost all wild and solitary. Add butterflies, moths, hoverflies, beetles, and — in the tropics — birds and bats. The "bee crisis" is really a crisis of an entire functional category.

## Why it's happening

There is no single cause. There is a sum of pressures acting together — and it's precisely the sum that makes the problem serious.

**Habitat loss.** The number one cause. Extensive monocultures, soil sealing and removed hedgerows erase flowers and nesting sites. A perfectly clean field, for a pollinator, is a desert.

**Pesticides.** In particular **neonicotinoids**, systemic insecticides that permeate the whole plant, nectar and pollen included. The evidence on their harmful effects led the European Union to **ban the outdoor use of three neonicotinoids** (imidacloprid, clothianidin, thiamethoxam) in 2018 — a decision based on EFSA's risk assessment.

**Parasites and diseases.** For the honey bee, the mite **Varroa destructor** is enemy number one: it attaches to bees, weakens them and transmits viruses. It is one of the drivers of the *Colony Collapse Disorder* (CCD) observed since the 2000s.

**Monoculture and poor diet.** A pollinator that has only one bloom available for a few weeks and then nothing is like a person eating a single food: it survives poorly.

**Climate change.** It shifts the timing: if the flower blooms before the pollinator is on the wing, the appointment is missed.

## What actually works

The good news is that pollinators respond quickly when the environment improves. The things that matter most are also the most concrete:

- **Diversity of blooms** throughout the season, not just in spring.
- **Edges left alive**: hedges, unmown meadows, "untidy" corners. It's the permaculture principle of edges — the place richest in life.
- **Reduce or eliminate systemic pesticides.**
- **Nesting sites**: dead wood, exposed bare earth, dry-stone walls. Most wild bees nest in the soil or in cavities, not in hives.

## Our piece of the solution

At Humus Sapiens the hives work inside a **Special Area of Conservation** with a rare flora: it's a context where wild pollinators still find what has disappeared elsewhere. But hosting them is not enough: the layered food forest, the staggered blooms, the edges left to grow and the restored dry-stone walls exist for them too. That's why, in the educational farm, pollinator biodiversity is one of the ten learning areas: protecting what pollinates is the most direct thing you can do for tomorrow's food.

You don't need a hectare to contribute. A pot of melliferous flowers on the balcony, a corner of the garden left wild, one less pesticide: at neighbourhood scale, these gestures added up count more than they seem.
