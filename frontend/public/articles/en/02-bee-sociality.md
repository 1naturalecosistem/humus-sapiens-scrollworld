# The sociality of bees: how a colony works

Open a hive and you face a city of 40,000–60,000 inhabitants at the height of summer. Nobody gives orders. The queen commands nothing. And yet the nest temperature stays constant, food is gathered, the young are fed, enemies are repelled. How does a society without a leader work better than almost all the ones with a leader?

The answer is that the honey bee (*Apis mellifera*) is a **eusocial** insect: the highest form of animal sociality. The colony is not a group of individuals, it is **a single diffuse organism** — a "superorganism", in the term made famous by biologists. A single bee counts as much as a cell in your body.

## Three castes, three destinies

**The queen.** One per colony. She is the only fertile female and has a single task: laying eggs, up to **1,500–2,000 a day** at the spring peak — more than her own body weight every day. She lives 2–5 years, against the few weeks of a worker. She doesn't "rule": she holds the colony together chemically, through pheromones.

**The workers.** All sterile females, they are 95% of the colony. They do everything else, and they do it by **changing job with age** (biologists call it *temporal polyethism*). In her ~6-week summer life a worker is, in order: cleaner, larva nurse, wax builder, storekeeper, guard at the entrance and, only in her last days, forager who flies out to gather. She dies, literally, working.

**The drones.** The males. They don't sting, don't forage, don't work. They exist for one purpose: to mate with a virgin queen of another colony, in flight. Those who succeed die in the act; the others, when autumn comes, are expelled from the hive — the colony wastes no food on useless mouths when the cold approaches.

## How they speak without words

Coordination arises from two languages.

**The bee dance.** When a forager finds a good bloom, she returns and "dances" on the comb. The Austrian biologist **Karl von Frisch** decoded this code and received the Nobel Prize for it in 1973. In the *waggle dance* the angle of the movement relative to the vertical indicates the **direction** of the food relative to the sun; the duration of the waggle indicates the **distance**. It's a danced GPS, translated for an audience in the dark.

**Pheromones.** These are chemical messages. The *queen mandibular pheromone* signals to the whole colony that there is a healthy, cohesive queen: when it fades, the workers realise they are orphaned and begin to raise a new queen. The alarm pheromone mobilises the guards in seconds. The colony "thinks" with chemistry.

## A body at 35 degrees

The most beautiful proof that the colony is a single organism is **thermoregulation**. The brood nest is kept around **35 °C**, precisely, all year round. When it's hot, the workers carry water and fan with their wings to evaporate it — a living air conditioner. When it's cold, they cluster tightly and vibrate their flight muscles to generate heat, rotating from outside to inside so none freezes. No individual decides: it is collective behaviour that produces stability.

## Why we look at the bees before anything else

On our ophiolitic soils the bees forage on a rare flora, and this gives the honey profiles found nowhere else. But for us the hive is above all **the most honest measuring instrument on the farm**. A bee explores square kilometres around the hive; what it brings back — or doesn't — tells the state of the flowers, the poisons, the local climate better than any sensor. When the colony thrives, the system around it is well. When it suffers, it's the first alarm bell, long before we would notice ourselves.

This is why, in the educational farm, we almost always start from the hive: understanding how 50,000 individuals build a single functioning body is the most direct way to understand what an ecosystem is.
