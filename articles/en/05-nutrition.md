# Nutrition: does the future lie in our roots?

There's an uncomfortable hypothesis that has circulated among agronomists for twenty years: today's apple is not your grandmother's apple. Same look, same name, perhaps bigger and shinier — but with less iron, less calcium, less vitamin C inside. If it were true, it would change the way we think about food: it would no longer be enough to "eat five portions of fruit and vegetables", because the portions wouldn't all be worth the same. Let's see what the data say, being honest about what is proven and what is still debated.

## The study that opened the case

The most cited reference is from 2004: **Donald Davis and colleagues** (*Journal of the American College of Nutrition*) compared US Department of Agriculture nutritional data for **43 vegetables and fruits**, between **1950 and 1999**. The result: statistically significant declines in **protein, calcium, phosphorus, iron, riboflavin (vitamin B2) and vitamin C**.

Why? The main hypothesis is the **"dilution effect"**: decades of genetic selection have rewarded varieties that yield more and grow faster. But a plant that produces more mass in the same time, on the same soil, often "dilutes" nutrients in a larger volume of water and carbohydrates. More yield, less density. It's not a conspiracy: it's an agronomic trade-off nobody had asked to measure on the nutritional side.

**Due honesty:** these studies have real limits (analysis methods changed over time, different varieties, non-identical sampling) and some downplay them. The decline is probably real but should be read without drama: it doesn't mean fruit and vegetables "no longer nourish", it means that *how much* they nourish depends on how and where they were grown.

## A second front: CO₂ in the air

Here the science is more recent and more solid. The rise in atmospheric carbon dioxide doesn't only inflate yields: **it impoverishes the composition of plants**.

A 2014 study in *Nature* (**Myers et al.**) showed that staple crops like wheat, rice and soy, grown at CO₂ concentrations projected for mid-century, contain **less zinc, less iron and less protein**. In parallel, the work of **Irakli Loladze** (*eLife*, 2014) described the same phenomenon on a broad scale: more CO₂ pushes the plant to produce more sugars and starches at the expense of minerals. In practice, we are "watering down" the planet's staple food, and this time the cause is atmospheric.

## The third front: ultra-processing

To the decline of the raw material we add what we do with it. The **NOVA** classification, developed by researchers at the University of São Paulo (Monteiro and colleagues), distinguishes foods by degree of processing, up to **ultra-processed** ones: industrial products recomposed from refined ingredients, additives, sugars and fats. A growing number of epidemiological studies associate high consumption of ultra-processed foods with greater health risks. The direction of the evidence is consistent enough to be taken seriously, even if — as always in nutrition — association is not automatically causation.

The overall picture: **less dense raw material + heavy processing = more calories, less nourishment**. We eat more and nourish less.

## Why "the roots" — and why it's not a slogan

The title isn't a gratuitous play on words. The nutritional density of a plant depends in large part on what it manages to **draw from the soil**, and this depends on the life of the soil: the network of fungi (mycorrhizae) and bacteria that connects roots to minerals. A soil worked to excess, fed only with a few chemical macro-elements and poor in organic matter, offers the plant a restricted menu. A living soil, rich in mycelium and organic matter, offers the whole pantry.

It is not yet precisely demonstrated *how much* regenerative agriculture increases micronutrients — the research is ongoing and this must be said clearly. But the logic is coherent and the first studies support it: **care for the soil and you care for what the soil puts on the plate**. It's the bridge between ecology and nutrition, and it's exactly the One Health principle — the health of soil, plant and person as a single system.

## What to take home

Without anxiety and without miracle supplements:

- **Variety and season** count more than quantity: different foods, picked ripe, cover more nutrients.
- **Short supply chain**: less time between harvest and plate means fewer nutrients lost along the way.
- **Less ultra-processed**, more recognisable food.
- **Ask how it was grown**, not just what it is. "Regenerative organic" and "supermarket organic" start from the same shelf but not from the same soil.

The future of nutrition, in all likelihood, is not in the next exotic superfood. It's under our feet — in the ability to put life back into the soil. In the roots, precisely.
